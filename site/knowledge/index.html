Knowledge: how the GB household energy market works

Poesys is a simulated GB energy supplier, living through the real 2016–2025 market, built to find households it can save money, time and carbon. These pages explain how the British household energy market really works, from published sources: the mechanisms the simulated world has to reproduce, and the rules the simulated supplier has to obey.

In brief

  • Each page explains one mechanism, from wholesale prices to debt collection, from published sources such as Ofgem, Elexon, DESNZ and the CMA.
  • This is what the project reads before it builds. A supplier capability is written up here first: what the rules require, what the evidence says, and what is not known.
  • Some of it is more than reading. Headline figures about the simulated supplier's customers, such as how many homes take both fuels and how many pay by direct debit, are written as claims with a published range. The simulated world is checked against them on every change that could move them and again every Monday, and a world that leaves the published range fails the build.
  • Where the published record does not settle a question, the page says so plainly rather than filling it with a guess.
  • The pages are grouped into five themes below. Each card says why the simulation needs it.

Markets and prices

Where a supplier's costs come from: the wholesale price, the costs added on top of it, and the cap on what it may charge.

GB electricity wholesale
How the GB wholesale electricity price is set at the margin.
Why the simulation needs it. Most of what a supplier pays for electricity is set here, so every tariff, margin and hedge the simulated company runs starts from this price.
GB gas wholesale
The GB gas market whose price sets the marginal plant's bid for most hours -- a market in its own right, not a driver-stub of electricity.
Why the simulation needs it. Gas sets the price of the marginal power station in most hours and is most of a gas-heated home's bill, so the 2021-22 crisis cannot be reproduced without it.
The GB electricity market (structure & settlement)
The half-hourly settled market these prices live in.
Why the simulation needs it. A supplier is settled half-hour by half-hour. The simulation keeps that clock so the company sees what a real supplier would see.
Merit order & residual demand
The cheapest-first dispatch queue and the demand left after renewables.
Why the simulation needs it. Explains why a windy hour is cheap and a still winter evening is dear: the daily shape the company's costs follow.
Imbalance & cash-out
How the system prices being short or long, up to the £6,000/MWh ceiling.
Why the simulation needs it. A supplier that buys too little or too much pays the system price for the difference. This is the cost of forecasting a book badly.
Hedging & the forward market
How a supplier buys ahead to blunt the price it would otherwise face.
Why the simulation needs it. Buying ahead is how a supplier survives a price spike. Many of the suppliers that failed in 2021-22 had not done it.
The non-commodity cost stack
Network and policy costs -- roughly a third of a bill, set administratively rather than cleared in a market.
Why the simulation needs it. Roughly a third of a bill is networks and policy, passed through at rates the supplier does not choose. Leave it out and every margin is wrong.
Why supplying a home costs more in some regions, under one national price
One wholesale price, fourteen network regions. Network charges differ by £14 to £44 per MWh between the cheapest and dearest region, and a supplier typically buys about a tenth more energy than its meters record because some is lost in the wires.
Why the simulation needs it. Network charges and losses are the regional cost a supplier carries under one national price. Leave losses out and the energy line is about a tenth light.
The retail price cap
How the wholesale price reaches the household through Ofgem's cap -- a retail boundary of the wholesale domain.
Why the simulation needs it. Ofgem's cap limits what most households can be charged, so it bounds every tariff the company can offer.
The price a household is shown
The cap headline and every comparison listing quote a year's money at an assumed consumption, for a tariff whose rate may not last the year.
Why the simulation needs it. A household deciding whether to switch compares a quoted annual figure, not its real bill. The company's offers have to be judged on the same terms.

Households and their homes

Who the customers are, what their homes use and how they behave. The simulated world has to reproduce this before any customer can be found in it.

How many synthetic households represent Britain
Why the sample size depends on strata rather than on variety, why adding a driver changes nothing, and what the current figure is still measured against.
Why the simulation needs it. Sets how many simulated homes it takes to stand for Britain, and why the groups that must be represented fix that number.
Who lives where: an address explains a tenth of who is inside, and the way a household pays marks where the risk is
A supplier knows a customer's address before it knows anything else. For the number of people inside, that address is worth surprisingly little. For money, the payment method a household chooses tells you more, and the reason it does is not the reason it looks like.
Why the simulation needs it. The first thing a supplier knows is an address. This says how little that reveals, and what reveals more.
What a house does to its energy use, and where the potential sits across Britain
Size, fabric and the fuel at the meter set most of what a home uses. Where it sits sets less than you would think. And the potential to save is not where the average home is.
Why the simulation needs it. Size, fabric and heating fuel set most of a home's demand. The simulated homes have to reproduce these published patterns.
Weather cells
How finely Britain has to be divided before every household's weather is described well enough to price -- and why the answer is a price list rather than a number.
Why the simulation needs it. Heating demand follows the weather, so each simulated home needs local weather fine enough to price, and no finer than it pays to be.
How houses change on their own
Boilers die and are replaced, solar panels and cars arrive, homes are extended — mostly without a supplier hearing about it.
Why the simulation needs it. Homes change on their own, usually without telling their supplier. The world has to change them so the company can be surprised, as a real one is.
What an energy supplier can see of a house
A supplier knows an address, a meter and a monthly total. Whether that house is worth an offer has to be inferred from that, and from an energy certificate that often does not exist.
Why the simulation needs it. The simulated company may use only what a real supplier could see. This page sets out what that is.
How a household chooses, and why most don't
Almost nobody deliberately chooses a standard variable tariff and most of the country is on one -- the stock is an outcome of behaviour, not an input to it.
Why the simulation needs it. Most households sit on the default tariff and few switch. The simulated households have to be as slow to move as real ones.
How households behave toward a supplier
How readily a household shops, how it likes to be contacted, whether it would rather have a stable bill or a cheaper one that moves with the clock, and whether it will let anyone control its battery or car. The scope is decided; the research and the build are not done.
Why the simulation needs it. How willing a household is to shop, to be contacted, or to let someone control its battery decides which offers can work at all.
How households change
Jobs are lost and found, babies arrive, children leave, people buy solar panels and electric cars without anyone selling them one. And a household that is glad to be saved money is not the same as one that will buy something. The scope is decided; the research and the build are not done.
Why the simulation needs it. Life events change what a household needs. A supplier that is to help has to notice them, so the world has to make them happen.
The first-year blind spot: how a shock count that could not see year one invented a loyalty pattern
In this project's simulated world, new customers looked more loyal than long-standing ones. Real GB evidence points the other way. All of the difference came from one measure that needed a year of history, and so read zero in every household's first year.
Why the simulation needs it. A worked case of one of the simulation's own measures misleading it, and how the real record exposed it.

Winning and serving customers

What a customer is worth, what it costs to win and keep one, and what to offer each. This is the method Poesys exists to automate.

What a customer is worth, and what drives it
Value is contribution multiplied by survival, which makes the drivers an order rather than a list: pays, stays, the size of the margin bet, then everything else.
Why the simulation needs it. The company's choice of whom to serve rests on what each customer is worth. This sets the order in which the drivers matter.
Winning a customer, and keeping one
Acquisition is a commission you pay someone else; retention is margin you give up yourself. Since 2022 a licence condition says so.
Why the simulation needs it. What it costs to win a customer and to keep one decides whether finding a particular household is worth doing.
How a household changes supplier: the switch, the objection, the save and the switch that goes wrong
A switch takes five working days under the Central Switching Service, and the losing supplier hears of it in time to object only for debt and to make a save offer only if the household itself cancels. About one switch in twenty met an objection before 2022, almost all for debt.
Why the simulation needs it. The world blocks an indebted household's switch and lets the company make a save on the loss notice; both are rules this page sets out, and the world has no switch that fails.
Choosing what to do for each customer, and when doing nothing is the best choice
Next best action means choosing, for one customer at one moment, the action that changes the outcome most. The question is not who is likely to leave or buy. Contact can make things worse, and the evidence says it often does.
Why the simulation needs it. Choosing what to offer each customer, and when to leave them alone, is the method Poesys is built to automate.
What a GB energy customer's satisfaction, complaints and recommendations are, and what they do
Satisfaction, NPS, sentiment, complaints, Ombudsman cases and trust are six different things. Complaining is something a household does; being dissatisfied is a state it is in. On the published record they point opposite ways on whether the household leaves.
Why the simulation needs it. Complaints, satisfaction and recommendations are different things that push a customer different ways. The company must not read one as another.
EV, solar and batteries: what a supplier gains, loses and can see
An electric car adds to what a household buys and solar panels take it away. A battery only moves it in time. They are three different products, and a supplier relates to each in three different ways.
Why the simulation needs it. Cars, panels and batteries change what a home buys and when it buys it. The company has to price each differently.
What happens to a supplier when a household moves home
A move is two events at two addresses, often weeks apart, and in Britain the energy keeps flowing to whoever walks in. Between a fifth and two-fifths of all domestic energy debt starts there.
Why the simulation needs it. A move closes one account and opens another, and a large share of energy debt starts there. The world has to move households for the company to meet that.

Metering, billing, debt and regulation

How energy used becomes money collected, and the rules that apply when it does not.

Metering and meter reads
The market settles every half hour; most meters are read a few times a year.
Why the simulation needs it. The market settles every half hour but most meters are read a few times a year. Estimated bills, catch-up bills and much debt come from that gap.
Energy that was used but never correctly billed
Every supplier pays for more energy than it bills. Some of the gap is just timing and closes by itself. Some of it is energy nobody will ever pay for. These are different things, and a supplier can only see some of them.
Why the simulation needs it. Every supplier pays for energy it never bills. Without that leakage the company's margins would look better than any real supplier's.
What a GB household's energy debt is, how big it has become, and how a supplier gets paid back
Energy debt is not one thing. A missed direct debit, a bill nobody opened, a prepayment meter quietly taking a few pounds a week, and an empty flat on a deemed contract all count as debt. They have different causes, different rules and very different chances of being repaid.
Why the simulation needs it. Debt is one of the largest costs a supplier carries. The company has to meet each kind of debt with the rules that govern it.
How a household pays for energy: direct debit, the other methods, and what a supplier owes the customer for getting the amount right
About seven in ten accounts pay by direct debit, and it is the cheap way to serve them. Getting the monthly amount right has been an absolute duty since 2022, and getting it wrong in either direction is what turns a payment method into bill shock, debt or a regulator's letter.
Why the simulation needs it. The payment method sets what a customer costs to collect (£57, £160 and £19 a year in the cap's debt allowance), and the simulated world draws only two methods.
Back bills: who pays for energy that was never charged for
Since May 2018 a supplier can only ask a household to pay for energy used in the twelve months before it asks. What counts as asking decides who carries the rest.
Why the simulation needs it. The twelve-month back-billing rule decides who pays for energy that was never charged for, and so what a late meter read costs the company.
Getting to the meter, and energy that is stolen
A supplier must try to read every meter once a year, but it cannot enter a home without consent or a warrant. It must look for theft, but finding theft costs it money. This page explains both duties, who pays when they fail, and what a supplier can see.
Why the simulation needs it. A supplier's duties to read meters and look for theft carry costs and limits the simulated company has to obey.
How a GB energy supplier accounts for the bills it will never collect
A provision is an estimate made today about money owed today. A write-off comes years later. The cost a supplier reports, and the cost the price cap allows, is the first, not the second.
Why the simulation needs it. How a supplier books money it will not collect decides the cost it reports. The company's accounts follow the same rule.
What a supplier must hold: revenue it has not billed, debt it will not collect, customers' money and the capital behind them
Revenue is earned as energy is used, about 5-8% of a year's revenue sits unbilled at year end, and since 31 March 2025 a supplier must hold £115 of adjusted net assets per dual-fuel customer, with customers' credit balances watched against its cash.
Why the simulation needs it. The company sizes its growth against the £115 Capital Target, and nothing in it yet watches its cash against customers' credit balances.

Carbon

Where the carbon behind a bill comes from, in the power market and in the home.

Where a gas-heated home's carbon comes from, and which measures save it
On today's grid the gas boiler is five to six times the carbon of all the home's electricity. The measures that save both money and carbon are mostly heating measures; the timing measures are small on both.
Why the simulation needs it. Saving customers carbon is one of the three things Poesys sets out to do. This page says where a home's carbon sits and which measures move it.
The UK carbon price (UK ETS + CPS)
The traded ETS price, charged per tonne emitted, plus Carbon Price Support, charged per unit of fuel burned for generation.
Why the simulation needs it. Carbon costs reach the wholesale price through the marginal plant, so they reach every bill, whatever the household thinks about carbon.
About the cards

The short codes beside it name the items in the project's build plan (called atoms internally) that build the mechanism the page describes.
