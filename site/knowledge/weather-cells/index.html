Weather cells

How finely Britain has to be divided before every household's weather is described well enough to price — and why the answer is a price list rather than a number.

A supplier's heat-load model needs to know what the weather is where each customer lives. It cannot carry 245,077 grid squares and it should not carry one national average. This page is the measurement of what lies between: …

1 — A cell is a climate class, not a region

The map below colours every 5 km square of Britain by which of 21 winter-temperature classes it belongs to, coldest to warmest. Patches of the same colour are scattered, and that is not a rendering fault. A cell is a set of places that behave alike, not a contiguous territory: a valley in Devon and a valley in Pembrokeshire can share a cell while the hill between them belongs to another.

This is why the cells are useless as an administrative geography and exactly right as a lookup. Nothing here follows a gas zone, a GSP group or a county.

The right-hand map is the reason the left-hand one looks complicated. The fine structure in any British weather map — the ridges, the exposed headlands, the upland edges — is almost all in the empty part. Weight by households and the picture simplifies sharply, because the variation a supplier is paid to resolve is not the variation the terrain produces.

Concentration, household-weighted: …

Two kinds of blank, and they are not the same thing.

Scotland is genuinely empty, and it was checked rather than assumed. Southern Scotland is … empty and the Highlands and north …, against 28% south of the Mersey. Two other explanations were tested and rejected: every remote town probed — Lerwick, Kirkwall, Stornoway, Thurso, Ullapool, Fort William, Oban, Braemar — carries households across dozens of cells, so the postcode-to-grid join has no holes north of the border; and Scotland's census join loses 0.21% of its households against 0.014% for England and Wales, which is real but far too small to show. Scotland holds 136 households per occupied km², between England's 252 and Wales's 115 — if cells were missing, households would pile into the survivors and that figure would be inflated.

Northern Ireland is not empty. It is not here. The Met Office grid this map is drawn on covers the whole United Kingdom, and … of its land cells lie outside Great Britain. The postcode directory carries no British grid reference for them and this company's market is GB, so they arrive with no household data at all. They are drawn in a colour of their own rather than as empty land, because a reader who took them for depopulated Britain would be learning something false.

3 — Granularity is a price list

Each line is the share of household-weighted variation captured by N cells. The lower line asks one partition to resolve all three heat-load drivers at once — winter temperature, wind and sunshine. The upper lines ask each driver on its own.

Coverage against cell count (log scale)
Solid: one shared partition across all three drivers. Dashed: each driver alone. Marked: 90%, 95% and 99% on the shared partition.
CoverageOne shared partitionPer driver, held separately

The last four points cost eleven times the cells the first ninety did. There is no natural cell count, only a price list — which is why the answer to “how many cells does Britain need?” is a curve. Asked one driver at a time, all three want about the same twenty-odd cells; the thousand is the price of forcing one grid to serve three jobs.

What this answers

How much granularity captures 90%, 95% or 99% of the variation in household heat-load drivers across Britain — and what each of those costs in cells.

What it does not

Level only. How long a cold spell lasts, and whether Glasgow and London are cold on the same day, are separate measurements — and the second one says these cells buy almost nothing in risk.
