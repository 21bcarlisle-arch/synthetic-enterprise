The seams — where the curtain is

The supplier never reaches into the world; it reads and writes through named doorways, the same way a real supplier reaches the industry. That boundary is the point of the design: going live means swapping a simulated counterparty for a real one behind an unchanged doorway, not rewriting the supplier. None of these connections is built — but they are not equally distant, so the table says what it would take to connect and whether the real specification has actually been read.

How solid the boundary is

The boundary is not a promise; it is measured, and the measurement is allowed to be unflattering. Every place the supplier's code and the world's code still touch directly is found by a walker, listed, and given a ruling — cut, or kept for a stated reason.

How the book grew, and what stopped it

The supplier starts with a handful of accounts and has to win the rest. A flat year on this table can mean four different things — it could not afford to quote, it chose not to grow faster than its own mandate, almost nobody was switching that year, or our own settlement engine refused to settle the accounts it won. The first three are facts about the supplier. The last is a limit of this simulation, and it is marked as one rather than left to look like a commercial result.

Does deciding customer-by-customer actually earn more?

The whole thesis is that a supplier which decides household by household beats one applying flat rules. That sentence only means something against a baseline, so the same book is run through the same world three times — once on flat rules, once on the per-customer decision engine, and once on a single flat margin set at the level the per-customer arm actually charged. The third run is what separates choosing better from charging more. Every figure below is what the world did, not what the company believed.
