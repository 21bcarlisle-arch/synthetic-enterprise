A simulated GB energy supplier, built and run by AI, living through ten years of the real market.

It settles every half hour of 2016–2025 against real Elexon and NESO data, the 2021–22 gas crisis included, and it can only learn what a real supplier could learn: meter reads, market feeds, customer contacts, bills and regulatory publications. It never sees the simulation's own internals. Its decisions are made by the code the AI wrote and maintains, not by a language model at run time: the company's code cannot reach the internet at all.

The mission: “We are creating enterprise value by automating ways to find individual customers we can create value for, and sharing in that value — by saving them money, time and carbon, through personalised modelling, tariffs and advice.”

And the project's own claim, beside it: that software can be grown and can maintain itself, rather than only be written. How it builds itself →

No customers. No licence. A running simulator -- a fully autonomous licensed supplier will not be permitted, because a licence needs accountable humans, and we say so up front. Autonomy is the how, not the headline.

What it has found

The book was 1% dual fuel. Published sources say about 72%.

On 17 October the director asked a plain question: how many of the founding households take electricity only, gas only, or both? The answer was 1% both. A coin set when the book was 13 accounts had been picking each founder's fuel on its own. The CMA's 2014 household survey puts 72% of all homes on one supplier for both fuels, and Ofgem's 2017 survey, the latest to publish the figure, puts 91% of homes with both on one supplier. Within a day the system researched the published record, rebuilt the founders as homes that buy both fuels where they have gas, and added checks that measure the routes into the book against those sources. The next published run was a dual-fuel book. The lesson was ours too: the question came from a person, and the checks exist so the next one will not have to. How households buy both fuels →

Is the profit from choosing better, or from charging more?

A supplier that prices each household individually and makes more money has not shown it created any value: it may simply have charged more. So the same book was run through the same world three ways: flat rules, the per-customer engine, and flat rules at the margin the engine actually charged. Only the gap between the last two is value made. The answer so far is we cannot tell yet, and the page says that rather than the flattering reading. The three runs and their spread →

When a home uses power changes its carbon.

Where the company holds a household's half-hourly meter reads, each half hour is multiplied by what the grid was actually burning in it. Few homes have them: in this decade almost every domestic meter was settled on a standard profile, not on its own half hours. Against the annual-average factor most footprints use, the same home's footprint moves, sometimes a long way on a single day. Carbon saved, which needs a counterfactual, is not claimed. Whose book this is

This book is households, and most of them take both electricity and gas, as most British homes do. The supplier below is real in the sense that matters here: it settles, prices, hedges, bills and collects against the actual market record. Every per-customer figure on this site is a figure about a household.

How the book came to be households, how it grows, and what sets its size

It was not always so. Until late August 2026 the book was a handful of industrial and commercial accounts carrying almost all of the revenue, so a household mission was being funded by a book that was not about households. The director suspended industrial and commercial supply. The capability is still built and still tested — half-hourly settlement, Triad curtailment, the flexibility revenue book, the DUoS/TNUoS split — but the company has stopped serving those accounts, which is a different thing from deleting them.

It grows because it is won. Each new account was quoted, credit-checked and carried through a statutory cooling-off period, and most quotes lost — there are years in the run that won nothing. Nothing is assigned to this company; a growth curve that cannot be lost is not a growth curve.

And its size is set by this machine, not by the market. The book starts from a set of founder households drawn when the run begins, and grows by what the company wins. How large it can be is limited by how much one run on this computer can settle, not by the supplier's capital or by demand. We would rather say so than let a small or flattening book read as a commercial result. The first measurement of that limit is written up in the repository, in the settlement-ceiling note of 24 August 2026. Account counts and per-segment economics are on Capabilities.

The claim, and what a household saves CARBON SAVED: NOT YET MEASURED

The claim, falsifiable: that you create value fastest by knowing each household well — shifting when it draws power, fitting the right assets, pricing the right tariff, having the right conversation — and that the value is real only if the household keeps a share of it. Run as a real autonomous supplier inside a faithful simulation of the actual UK market. Every line of company code has to answer one question: could a real supplier know this?

A household is saved money, time and carbon. Carbon's job is to show each household what it has saved and could save in CO₂. Inside the company, the same saving is also priced in £ per tonne of CO₂e saved, against the UK government's carbon appraisal value, £273/tCO₂e (2025), in 2022 prices. That price is a diagnostic, not the headline.

The money side has been run: we cannot tell yet. Time is not yet measured, only one of the three ways to reach a household (tariffs) works today, and carbon saved is not yet measured. Each is set out below, in full.

The full account: the three runs, what is and is not built, and why carbon saved is not yet measured

And the money side of that claim has now been run, with this result: we cannot tell yet. The same book was put through the same world three times — on flat rules, on the per-customer decision engine, and on a single flat margin set at the level the per-customer arm actually charged. That third run is what separates choosing better, which would be value made, from charging more, which only moves it. In the world as now fitted to the published switching record, the choosing leg was re-drawn three times over nothing but each household's hidden price sensitivity, by the code this page is published from. It came out above zero on all three draws, but only one of the three clears the spread between them, so a direction would rest on which draw the run happened to make, and this page states none. Most of the advantage the book does show is the price level, which is value moved rather than made. The claim above remains the claim. It is not yet the finding. The three runs, the split and the three-draw band →

Two of the three currencies, and two of the three channels, are not built. Only money has ever been optimised here, and only our side of it. What a household saved by being with us is now measured — against the published default tariff, at that household's own metered volumes — but it is a diagnostic and nothing yet decides anything by it. That is a different question from the money result above: this compares the household with the default tariff, not our pricing with simpler rules. Time is defined but not yet measured. It means a household's effort, counted in its own unit (household minutes for each kind of task: shopping around, sorting out a problem, recovering from one, routine admin) and never turned into pounds. Nothing in the run counts those minutes yet. And of the three ways value is meant to reach a household — personalised modelling, tariffs, advice — only tariffs reaches one today: the modelling that exists is the supplier's opinion about its customers rather than a model offered to them, and the advice code has no recipient. Said here rather than in a footnote, because a mission with two thirds of its channels unbuilt is the kind of thing a company usually declines to mention.

The honest state, plainly. The half-hourly instrument now runs: where a household has half-hourly meter reads, they are multiplied by what the grid was actually burning in each half hour. That is emissions, and emissions are measurable. See it on a real household →

Why carbon saved still says NOT YET MEASURED. Abatement is not emissions. It is what a household did not emit because of something we did, which needs a claim about a world that did not happen — a randomised holdout, a matched comparison, or a weather-normalised before-and-after. At the current number of half-hourly reads none of those is viable, and the honest thing is to say so rather than publish an engineering estimate with a measurement's confidence. The number that would change this fastest is not a model: it is meters.

The worldBuilding

Real weather over 2016–2025 drives the actual half-hourly wholesale prices — 168,000 settlement periods, including the 2021–22 gas crisis. That price record is real. Per-home demand is part-way there. Where the weather store covers a household's location, its electricity is drawn half hour by half hour from a thermal model of its own building under its own local weather, and its gas is split between heating and everything else by the same model. Homes outside that coverage still use one national shape rescaled, so they look too smooth, and a crowd of them does not vary the way a real street does.

What the company can seeLive

The company never sees inside the simulation (the diagram calls this boundary the wall). It learns its world only the way a real supplier would — market feeds, forecasts that carry error, imperfect meter reads, payments, complaints — and the settled truth about a period lands months after it happened.

The companyLive

One joined-up stack: win customers, forecast the book, price tariffs, hedge forward, bill on three clocks, chase what’s owed, settle late and learn from the true-up. Billing, collections, cost-to-serve and the discovery loop run today, and renewal prices are now held under a published competitor signal rather than set in a vacuum; the cash-collateral death-loop is still to come. It can be wrong, and it must not be guaranteed to live — but nothing in the world can yet push it under.

The scoreBuilding

Three tests: survive a worst-case shock, earn on a probability-weighted basis, and abate — cut carbon per household, priced in £ per tonne. Survive and earn run today. Household emissions are measured: a home’s half-hourly reads, where the company has them, against what the grid was actually burning. Abatement, meaning what a household did not emit because of something we did, is not yet measured, and the site says so rather than pretend.

Two ways in

If you work in energy

What is modelled faithfully today, what is not yet, and how each compares with how a real GB supplier works. Each line links to the page that sources it.

Settlement
Faithful today: every half hour is settled against the real published Elexon system price, imbalance is a real cost line, and the settled truth reaches the company months late, as it does in the industry.
Not yet: Supplier Volume Allocation and the reconciliation run calendar are modelled by their effect on the figures, not in their full detail. In 2016–2025 almost every home was settled on a standard profile (98.5% of meters in May 2022, by Elexon's count). Half-hourly settlement for homes began only in late 2025, after the record ends. The run settles each home's own half hours, so it charges a supplier for timing that one of the period was not charged for. How much that is worth is not yet measured.
The price cap
Faithful today: the cap limits unit rates and standing charges, not the bill, and its headline describes a typical home at typical use. Both are checked against Ofgem's own material.
Not yet: the cap's published levels are not yet ingested as a series, and the wholesale allowance's method has not been read line by line.
Hedging
Faithful today: the company hedges to a mandate, layered across terms, and its realised cost is reported against going unhedged, including the calm years in which the hedge lost money and the 2021–22 crisis it was bought for.
Not yet: no options, no trading views and no cross-commodity hedging; collateral is a cash constraint, and the counterparty credit process is simplified.
Debt
Faithful today: billing and collections run every month, and the world's stock of debt more than 91 days old matches what Ofgem publishes.
Not yet: write-offs per pound billed are well below a large supplier's, the world generates no prepayment debt, and its payment-failure rates are not yet sourced.
Home moves
Faithful today: a household that moves keeps its region at least as often as the published lower bound, and its new home's use comes from the new dwelling, not the old one.
Not yet: only the electricity supply follows the household; the gas supply ends at the move. The value of a mover offer, time on a deemed contract and an empty home's use are gaps in the published record, so each is reported as a range.
Dual fuel
Faithful today: most homes with gas buy both fuels from one supplier, and the book now draws its households that way from the CMA and Ofgem figures; a dual-fuel account's gas and electricity are one house, not two.
Not yet: the move rule above, so a dual-fuel household that moves leaves as an electricity-only one.
