Can AI build and run an energy supplier on its own, and find value for each household?

Poesys is a simulated GB energy supplier, built and run by AI, living through ten years of the real market. It settles every half hour of the real market record, from 2016 to June 2025, against Elexon and NESO data, the 2021–22 gas crisis included. Its decisions are made by the code the AI wrote and maintains, not by a language model at run time.

The mission: “We are creating enterprise value by automating ways to find individual customers we can create value for, and sharing in that value — by saving them money, time and carbon, through personalised modelling, tariffs and advice.”

And the project's own claim, beside it: that software can be grown and can maintain itself, rather than only be written. How it builds itself →

A whole supplier, end to end

The core functions of a GB household supplier, each tested, each linked to the page that shows it. What it does not do yet is listed, in full, on Capabilities.

Winning customers
Quotes each household, checks its credit, carries it through the 14-day cooling-off period and the change of supplier, objections included. Most quotes lose, and the book is only what it wins.
Pricing under the cap
Prices fixed and variable tariffs at each renewal, and from the cap's start in January 2019 holds every default tariff's unit rate and standing charge at or under Ofgem's published level, recomputed from the cap series itself.
Hedging the wholesale book
Buys forward to a mandate, layered across terms, and reports the realised cost against going unhedged, in the calm years and through the crisis.
Half-hourly settlement
Settles every half hour: the hedged share at its forward price, the rest at the real Elexon system price, each home bought on its registered profile class and trued up to its own volume, with the settled truth arriving months late, as it does in the industry.
Billing
Bills every account monthly on one customer ledger, and reconciles every invoice and payment to it.
Direct debits set and reviewed
Sets each household's first direct debit from an estimate of its year, reviews it on the licence's terms (SLC 27.15), and from January 2021 lets a household's credit on one fuel offset its debt on the other (SLC 27.8D).
Collections and debt
Ages what is owed, chases it, writes off what is lost, and carries an expected-loss provision drawn from a forward forecast of every account's cash (IFRS 9).
Back-billing and read duties
Never back-bills a household for energy used more than twelve months before the bill when the delay was the supplier's (SLC 21BA), and instructs a meter visit when a year passes on estimates, as the read duty requires (SLC 21B.4).
Capital behind each customer
Plans its growth against Ofgem's decided Capital Target: none before the July 2023 decision, then £115 of adjusted net assets per dual-fuel customer, binding from 31 March 2025.
Household carbon, per half hour
Multiplies each half hour of a home's electricity, where it has the reads, by what the grid was actually burning in it, and adds its gas at the published combustion factor, so a household can see its own footprint move.

A world it cannot see into

Behind the supplier is a synthetic Britain. Its households carry traits the company is never told: how sensitive each is to price, how it pays, how green it is. Each home has its own building physics, heated under the weather of its own place. Lives change: homes take on heat pumps, solar and electric cars, and households move. All of it plays out against the real market record.

The company learns that world only as a real supplier would: meter reads, market feeds, payments, complaints and published regulation, and nothing else. A wall in the code stops it reading the simulation's own truth, and the company's code cannot reach the internet at all. The book it serves is households, most of them on both fuels, as most British homes are.

The worldBuilding

The actual half-hourly wholesale prices of 2016–2025 are replayed as they happened — 168,000 settlement periods, including the 2021–22 gas crisis. Real weather drives each home's demand, not the prices. Per-home demand is part-way there. Where the weather store covers a household's location, its electricity is drawn half hour by half hour from a thermal model of its own building under its own local weather, and its gas is split between heating and everything else by the same model. Homes outside that coverage still use one national shape rescaled, so they look too smooth, and a crowd of them does not vary the way a real street does.

What the company can seeLive

The company never sees inside the simulation (the diagram calls this boundary the wall). It learns its world only the way a real supplier would — market feeds, forecasts that carry error, imperfect meter reads, payments, complaints — and the settled truth about a period lands months after it happened.

The companyLive

One joined-up stack: win customers, forecast the book, price tariffs, hedge forward, bill on three clocks, chase what’s owed, settle late and learn from the true-up. Billing, collections, cost-to-serve and the discovery loop run today, and renewal prices are now held under a published competitor signal rather than set in a vacuum; the cash-collateral death-loop is still to come. It can be wrong, and it must not be guaranteed to live — but nothing in the world can yet push it under.

The scoreBuilding

Three tests: survive a worst-case shock, earn on a probability-weighted basis, and carbon — the CO₂ each household saved and could save (£ per tonne stays an internal diagnostic). Survive and earn run today. Household emissions are measured: a home’s half-hourly reads, where the company has them, against what the grid was actually burning. Abatement, meaning what a household did not emit because of something we did, is not yet measured, and the site says so rather than pretend.

A question showed its world was wrong. It fixed it within a day.

On 17 October the director asked how many founding households take electricity only, gas only, or both. The answer was 1% both. A coin set when the book was 13 accounts had been picking each founder's fuel on its own. The CMA's 2014 household survey puts 72% of all homes on one supplier for both fuels, and Ofgem's 2017 survey, the latest to publish the figure, puts 91% of homes with both on one supplier. Within a day the system researched the published record, rebuilt the founders as homes that buy both fuels where they have gas, and added checks that measure the routes into the book against those sources.
