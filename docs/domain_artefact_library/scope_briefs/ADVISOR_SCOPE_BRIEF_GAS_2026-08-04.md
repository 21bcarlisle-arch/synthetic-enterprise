## A. What makes gas structurally different from electricity

Electricity cannot be stored, so it must balance instantaneously and price by merit order. **Gas can be stored, and the pipeline network itself is a store.** That single fact changes everything downstream: the market balances over a day rather than a half hour, inventory carries value between seasons, and price is set by the cost of the marginal *molecule* — the next available supply source — rather than by a dispatch stack.

A complete treatment answers: **why is this a different market from electricity, not a variant of it?**

## B. Products and structure

**The gas day runs 05:00 to 05:00** — not midnight, and not the electricity day.

**The traded ladder:** within-day (same-day delivery), day-ahead (the most referenced short-term price), then months, quarters, **summers (April–September) and winters (October–March)**, and annual contracts — with the **gas year running October to September**, distinct from the calendar year.

**Where it trades:** over the counter directly, through brokers, and on exchanges — futures on ICE. **Quoted in pence per therm**, roughly 29.3 kWh, which is a real conversion the model must get right rather than assume.

**The balancing market is a distinct venue.** The On-the-day Commodity Market is an anonymous cleared platform used for balancing; from mid-afternoon on the delivery day the system operator becomes counterparty to every trade. Minimum trade sizes apply. **Cash-out is not a penalty regime:** a shipper long or short is cashed out at marginal system buy or sell prices which track the spot market — efficient rather than punitive, which is why daily liquidity is high.

A complete treatment answers: **what can be bought, over what day, in what unit, and what happens to a position left unbalanced?**

## C. Mechanism — how the price is formed

**Supply stack, not merit order.** Price is set by the marginal source needed to meet demand: UK production, Norwegian pipeline, continental interconnectors, storage withdrawal, or LNG. **The marginal source changes with the season and with global conditions** — and when LNG is marginal, the UK price is set by global competition for cargoes rather than by anything domestic.

**Linepack — the pipeline as a battery.** Gas stored within the transmission system fluctuates through the day. Opening linepack is measured at the start of the gas day; shippers are incentivised to put in what their customers take out. **This is real intraday flexibility with a physical limit**, and it is why the gas market tolerates within-day imbalance in a way electricity cannot.

**Storage as an inventory problem.** Injection in summer, withdrawal in winter, with the summer–winter spread determining whether storing is economic. **The spread is an output of expectations, not an input** — and in 2022 it *inverted*, which is the single sharpest test of whether a gas model is formed or scripted.

**Cash-out and the balancing incentive** as above.

A complete treatment answers: **which source is marginal today, and what would make a different one marginal tomorrow?**

## D. Drivers

- **Weather** — heating demand, the dominant seasonal driver; and cold *and still* raises gas demand twice, once for heat and once for gas-fired generation replacing absent wind.
- **Gas-for-power** — low wind lifts gas burn, so the electricity market is a *demand driver* for gas at the same moment gas is a *cost driver* for electricity. **The coupling runs both ways** and a model with only one direction is wrong.
- **Global LNG competition** — if Asia outbids Europe, UK prices rise through scarcity of cargoes. Traders watch arrival schedules and regasification rates.
- **Storage levels**, European as well as domestic, since the UK is interconnected.
- **Continental prices** — TTF is the European benchmark; flows reverse to follow price, so continental spikes pull UK prices up via exports.
- **Supply disruption and geopolitics** — priced as a risk premium in forwards, which is sentiment moving *real* prices through flows, not merely beliefs.
- **Declining domestic production**, raising import dependence over the decade.

A complete treatment answers: **which of these are observable, which are inferred, and which arrive as shocks?**

## E. Structure over time

Strong seasonality with winter far above summer. The **summer–winter spread** as the storage signal. Regimes: pre-2021, the crisis, and after — where level, volatility and correlation all move together. And the decade-long structural shift from domestic production toward imports and LNG.

A complete treatment answers: **what does a normal gas year look like, and what did 2021–22 change that was not simply "bigger"?**

## F. Modellable versus random

Fundamentals that should be **caused**: seasonal demand from weather, storage injection and withdrawal economics, the supply stack and which source is marginal, linepack within its physical bounds. Residual that should be **drawn** with honest tails: cargo diversions, outages, geopolitical shocks, weather beyond forecast horizon.

**The storage trajectory must be an output, not a path.** A scripted trajectory can only ever produce the crisis its author imagined.

## G. Disqualification battery

1. Gas treated as a correlated series alongside power rather than the thing setting power's marginal cost.
2. No storage, or storage as a fixed schedule rather than an economic decision.
3. The summer–winter spread as an input rather than an emergent result — and **incapable of inverting.**
4. No linepack, so no within-day flexibility.
5. LNG absent, or present without global competition for cargoes.
6. Cash-out modelled as a penalty rather than at marginal system prices.
7. The gas day starting at midnight.
8. Therms and kilowatt-hours confused, or the conversion assumed.
9. Only one direction of gas–power coupling.
10. No interconnector reversal — flows fixed in one direction.
11. One regime across the decade.
12. Domestic production static across ten years.
