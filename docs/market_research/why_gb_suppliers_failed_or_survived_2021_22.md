# Why GB domestic suppliers failed or survived in 2021–22, and which strategies the published record can define

**Knowledge:** suppliers-accounts-and-capital

*Delivery seat, 18 Nov 2026. Step 1 (K8) of the director's steer of 18 Nov,
`docs/staging/advisor/done/DIRECTOR_STEER_THE_WORLD_MUST_RANK_REAL_2021_22_SUPPLIER_STRATEGIES_IN_THE_ORDER_REALITY_DID_2026-11-18.md`.
The steer asks: run through our world's real 2021–22, do stylised versions of real supplier strategies fail and survive in
the order they did in reality? This page sets out what reality did, from the published reviews, before any strategy is
defined or any run is made. Firms are named only where the reviews name them. No individual is named.*

## What happened (the outcome being ranked)

- **29 suppliers failed between July 2021 and May 2022**, "affecting nearly four million households" (NAO, report page).
  28 went through Supplier of Last Resort, moving "nearly 2.4 million customers". Bulb Energy, with about 1.6 million
  customers, went into special administration on 24 November 2021 (NAO 1.13, 2.20).
- **Cost:** Ofgem's estimate is £2.7bn, about £94 per customer, mostly to buy wholesale energy above the cap for
  transferred customers (NAO summary). The government spent £0.9bn running Bulb in 2021-22, and £1.0bn is budgeted for
  2022-23 (NAO 2.21–2.22).
- **Who failed:** "With the exception of Bulb Energy, all of those who exited the market were small or medium-sized
  suppliers" (NAO 3.22). Between Dec 2010 and May 2022 the number of suppliers went from 12 to 23, with a peak of 70 in
  mid-2018 (NAO report page).
- **Who survived:** Oxera's comparison group of suppliers still active in March 2022 is EDF and British Gas (large
  legacy), OVO and Octopus (large non-legacy), Opus Energy and ESB Energy (medium), and Dual Energy and BES Utilities
  (small) (Oxera p.44–45). Its failed sample is Bulb and Avro (large non-legacy), Utility Point, Pure Planet and People's
  Energy (medium), and CNG Energy, PFP Energy and Green Supplier (small). That sample covers 82% of SoLR levy claims
  for Sept–Dec 2021 (Oxera fn 14).

## Why they failed: what the reviews establish

**1. Every failure in Oxera's sample combined several weaknesses. None of them alone predicts failure.** This is the
review's central finding, and the one that matters most for a ranking test:

> "while all the factors discussed above can be used as leading indicators for supplier failures, none of these
> individual factors alone predict failure. Instead, it is the combination of multiple factors that has reduced
> suppliers' abilities to absorb external shocks" (Oxera p.58).

The four factors (Oxera p.12, p.57):

1. negative equity balances in the years leading up to failure;
2. poor liquidity, meaning low current ratios and low working capital;
3. over-reliance on customer credit balances to finance operations;
4. "either unhedged, or not substantively (i.e. more than 50% over nine months or more) hedged, positions".

"**All** failed companies had negative and deteriorating equity balances in the years leading up to their failure"
(p.57). "With the exception of CNG Energy, the other failed suppliers had at least two areas that limited their ability
to absorb shocks" (p.57). CNG's failure "is attributed mainly to the loss of key customers, which accounted for c. 80% of
its customer base" (p.57). That is a demand shock, not a strategy.

**2. Hedging alone did not separate failed suppliers from survivors.**

> "failed companies had lower willingness to report hedging arrangements prior to failure, and those that did report
> had, on average, poorer hedging arrangements than those that are still active. However, hedging alone is not
> sufficient for determining a supplier's propensity to fail, as shown by the failed suppliers that were relatively well
> hedged, and some active suppliers that had relatively low levels of hedging and/or were hedged over a relatively
> short horizon" (Oxera p.56).

- Of the 15 failed suppliers that disclosed their hedging, **seven were unhedged or hedged under six months**. Three
  (Ampower, Avro, GOTO) hedged beyond nine months but below 50% at that horizon. **At least four (Together Energy, PFP
  Energy, Simplicity Energy, Orbit Energy) hedged over 50% at nine months or more** (Oxera p.110).
- NAO: "We reviewed the administrators' reporting … for 24 out of the 29 suppliers … and found that **17 (71%) mention
  insufficient hedging**" (NAO 3.2). "Of the 24 administrators' reports … **18 (75%) mentioned the price cap** as a
  contributing factor" (NAO 3.21).

**3. Two business models were named, but how widely they were used was never measured** (Oxera p.58–59; NAO 3.3):

- **The timing model:** "suppliers undercut hedged rivals by entering at favourable moments in the market, entering into
  long-term supply agreements with customers based on prevailing low spot rates. By not sufficiently hedging, this
  strategy eventually leads to failure if spot prices increase persistently."
- **The growth model:** "businesses relied on receiving customer balances prior to the provision of services … They then
  relied on growth in the customer base to keep ahead of future liabilities, making the strategy unsustainable in the
  long term during times when growth slows down."
- "it is not possible to determine the extent to which they were used because of limits in the information Ofgem holds"
  (NAO 3.3). **GAP:** how many failed suppliers ran each model.
- Supporting evidence: credit balances held across the year stayed at about 40% of the year's bill, persistently,
  "Rather than fluctuating around 0, as might be expected by suppliers that use only direct debits to smooth bills"
  (Oxera p.59, from Ofgem 2018 analysis). The Renewables Obligation, collected all year and paid each August, also
  worked "as a form of temporary working capital" (NAO 3.6).

**4. The price cap's six-month lag turned a price rise into a loss on every default-tariff unit.**

> "suppliers make windfall losses when rising wholesale prices increase above the level assumed when the cap was set
> … when wholesale prices rise, the SVT acts as a ceiling and suppliers are exposed to the full differential between
> wholesale costs and the price cap" (Oxera p.38).
> "from late 2020 onwards, the direct fuel component of the price cap is below the other measures of wholesale costs.
> This means that suppliers — even those who are hedging for six-month periods — have paid more for energy than they
> are allowed to charge customers" (Oxera p.39).

A default-tariff book is hard to hedge because its customers can leave at any time. Together Energy, a well-hedged
supplier that failed in January 2022, told Ofgem: "Our current SVT hedge profile steps down over 6 months, 100% of SVT
volume hedged in the month ahead, to 20% in month 6 … A customer signing up to our SVT today will produce negative gross
margins for Together Energy" (Oxera p.109). Oxera: "despite owning a relatively well-hedged portfolio, the combination of
demand uncertainties and rising wholesale costs contributed to the insolvency of Together Energy."

**5. Fixed tariffs.** "as at April 2021 the majority of these had more than half their customer base on a fixed-price
tariff. This could have limited these suppliers' capacity to put up their prices … even without the price cap
existing" (NAO 3.22). **GAP:** the fixed share for each supplier, and for the survivors.

**6. What the survivors did** (Oxera p.56–57), qualitatively only:

- "entering into long-term supply agreements with wholesale suppliers";
- "passing on the cost of hedging to customers";
- "hedging a significant proportion of demand exposure over an extended period of time (most active suppliers)".

## What the published record licenses as "the order reality did"

Read with F11, so wherever the evidence is ambiguous the conservative reading is taken:

1. **Established:** a book with **negative equity, high reliance on credit balances, and hedging under 50% at nine
   months** failed in autumn 2021 (Sep–Oct). Every firm in Oxera's sample that matches all three failed.
2. **Established:** **a well-hedged book with negative equity and a default-tariff book it could not hedge also
   failed**, later (PFP Sep-21; Orbit Dec-21; Together Jan-22). So hedging delays failure at best; it does not prevent
   it when equity is negative. **A world in which strong hedging alone guarantees survival contradicts reality.**
3. **Established only as a binary:** suppliers that hedged a significant share over an extended period, held long-term
   supply agreements or passed hedging costs on survived to March 2022. **GAP:** their parameters.
4. **Not established:** an order of failure *timing* within the failed group that follows from strategy. The failure
   months bunch (Sep–Oct 2021) and depend on events no strategy parameter carries: the Sep-21 gas spike, the Oct-21 cap
   reset, and when each SoLR was appointed. Firms with very different hedging, from Green Supplier at 0% to PFP at 91%,
   failed in the same month.

**So the ranking reality supports is two-tier, not a full order:** {weak on several of the four factors} fail, and
{positive equity and substantive hedging} survive. Within the failed tier the evidence gives only a weak tendency:
unhedged books fail no later than well-hedged ones. A test that demanded a strict order of five strategies would be
testing a precision reality does not have.

Under the steer's rule ("a strategy with too many GAPs is dropped, with the reason"), **S5, the only survivor
strategy, cannot be defined from the reviews read.** Without a survivor, the test can check only that the world kills
S1–S4. It cannot check that the world separates them from a strategy reality spared. A world that killed every supplier
would pass. **This is the test's binding gap**, and it is named here before any run.

## Our design's choices, and what is left open

- **The instrument runs stylised strategies as a harness tool, not as the company (W7, R13).** The company never sees
  them or their results.
- **The world's half is what is being tested:** the 2021–22 wholesale path, the cap's six-month reset, and the
  default-tariff customer's freedom to leave. A strategy's own balance sheet (equity, credit balances, a hedge ladder) is
  stated by the instrument from this page and is not drawn by the world.
- **Not yet established: whether the world can run a stylised supplier's cash path at all**, outside the company. That
  is a discovery question about our code, for the next step, not a domain question.
- **Equity levels for S1–S4:** the reviews give only the sign. Any level below zero is consistent with them. The
  instrument should sweep a range below zero rather than pick a point, and report whether the verdict depends on it.

## Gaps, in one list
